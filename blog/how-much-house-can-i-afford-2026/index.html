How Much House Can I Afford in 2026? The Answer Moved $27,000 Without You Noticing

October 9, 2026 · 9 min read · By Particulate LLC

Here's the uncomfortable math of October 2026: the 30-year fixed rate averaged 7.40% last week (Freddie Mac PMMS, Oct 8) — up from 6.30% a year ago. If you make $95,000 with $450/month in existing debts, that one-point move quietly deleted about $27,000 of buying power. Same income, same down payment, same house hunt — a different number on the pre-approval.

Most "how much house can I afford" articles stop at a multiple of your salary ("3x income!") — advice that was already wrong in 2019 and is useless now. What actually determines your number is a three-way negotiation between your income, your debts, and the rate on the day you lock. Where Your Number Actually Comes From

Lenders don't care about the house price. They care about two ratios, computed on gross monthly income:

  • Front-end DTI: your total housing payment (principal + interest + property tax + insurance + HOA + mortgage insurance) ÷ monthly income. Standard cap: 31%.
  • Back-end DTI: housing payment plus car payments, student loans, credit card minimums ÷ monthly income. Standard cap: 43% (with strong compensating factors, some approvals stretch toward 50%).

For our example buyer — $95,000 income, $450/month in debts — that's a housing budget of $2,454/month (31% front-end binds first). Then the calculator solves for the biggest loan that payment supports at your rate, after property tax and insurance eat their share. At 7.40% with $40,000 down and $500/month in property taxes, that's:

The Rate Paradox: Same Buyer, One Year Apart

Here's what the last twelve months did. The Freddie Mac survey rate went from 6.30% (Oct 2025) to 7.40% (Oct 2026). Run the same buyer — same $95K income, same $40K down — at both rates:

The payment didn't move — the house did. Your DTI caps fix the monthly budget; the rate decides how much house that budget buys. A full point of rate took about 10% off your ceiling. This is why "how much can I afford" has no timeless answer: it expires with the rate on the day you lock.

The other direction matters just as much, for anyone deciding whether to wait for lower rates: the same $247,000 loan costs $1,710/month at 7.40% but $1,545 at 6.40%. Over 30 years, that one point is about $59,450 in lifetime interest ($368,585 vs $309,134). Waiting for a rate you can't predict has a real carrying cost too — there is no free option here, and anyone who tells you they know where rates go next is selling something.

2026 Loan Limits and the Three Paths

Three common loan types, three different rulebooks. The calculator's comparison table runs all three on your numbers:

  • Conventional (conforming): 2026 FHFA limit is $832,750 baseline ($1,249,125 in high-cost counties). PMI if you put down under 20% — and here's the part people miss: PMI is not forever. Once you reach 20% equity (through payments or appreciation), conventional PMI drops off. At our example numbers that's $119/month back in your pocket eventually.
  • FHA: floor limit $541,287. Lower credit thresholds, but the mortgage insurance is a double charge: 1.75% upfront (financed into the loan, so it compounds) plus 0.55%/yr for the life of the loan if you start above 90% LTV. Life of the loan — the only exit is refinancing to conventional after you build equity.
  • Jumbo: above conforming limits. Stricter reserves-and-credit requirements; at 7.4% rates, the payment math on a large jumbo is unforgiving — the calculator will cap the loan at the high-cost ceiling of $1,249,125 and show DTI going red fast.

At our example numbers, FHA and conventional come out nearly identical monthly ($114 vs $119 of MI), but FHA costs about $4,362 more cash to close because of the upfront MIP. For a buyer with strong credit and 10% down, conventional usually wins on total cost. FHA wins when the credit score or down payment is thin — that's what it exists for.

The 15-Year Trade-Off Nobody Prices Correctly

Switch the term to 15 years in the calculator. Same buyer, same $2,454 budget: the max loan collapses to $188,638 (a $228,638 target price) — because at 7.4% rates, the same payment amortizes over half the time. That's $58,000 less house.

The 15-year loan is a fantastic idea if the house you want fits under that number anyway — on that loan size you'd pay about $124,200 in lifetime interest versus $281,600 on the 30-year path, a savings of roughly $157,400. But most first-time buyers in a high-cost state like New Jersey hit the DTI wall first, and the honest move is the 30-year note with a plan to make extra principal payments in months you can afford it. Discipline you control beats a term that binds you.

What This Calculator Leaves Out

Intentionally, to stay honest about what it can estimate:

  • Points, locks, and lender fees — these live inside your rate quote and closing costs, which is why the closing % field is adjustable (NJ commonly lands 2–4%).
  • Escrow cushion and prepaid items — your first property-tax quarter and a year of insurance typically get collected at closing. Add roughly one month of housing payment to cash-to-close for this.
  • NJ-specific costs already in the fields: New Jersey's effective property taxes are the highest in the country — the default $500/month is a starter; many NJ towns run $800–$1,200/month on a $300K house. Run your actual township rate or your number will be too optimistic.
  • Appreciation, refinancing odds, and rate futures — unknowable, and any calculator that claims otherwise is lying to you.
