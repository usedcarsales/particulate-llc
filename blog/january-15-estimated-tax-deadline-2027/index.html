The January 15 Estimated Tax Deadline: The Payment That Ends Your Tax Year

October 18, 2026 · 6 min read · By Particulate LLC

Most people think their tax year closes on April 15. It doesn't. If you're self-employed, a landlord, a gig worker, or anyone else who earns income without an employer withholding taxes for you, your 2026 tax year closes on January 15, 2027 — the due date for the fourth-quarter estimated tax payment.

Here's what that deadline actually requires, how the safe harbors protect you, what it costs to miss, and how to check your numbers in two minutes.

The one-sentence version: the January 15, 2027 deadline is the last chance to pay timely estimated tax for tax year 2026. Payments made after it (or at filing in April) still count toward the bill — but they're late, and the underpayment penalty has already been running on every dollar you were short along the way.

Who Owes a Q4 Payment on January 15

Individuals — including sole proprietors, partners, and S corporation shareholders — generally must make estimated tax payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when their 2026 return is filed, after subtracting withholding and refundable credits. If a paycheck (or pension) already withholds enough to cover your tax, you're not in this system. If you have meaningful income with nothing withheld, you are.

The four 2026 quarterly due dates for individuals:

QuarterCovers income earnedPayment due
Q1January – MarchApril 15, 2026
Q2April – MayJune 15, 2026
Q3June – AugustSeptember 15, 2026
Q4September – DecemberJanuary 15, 2027

Note the odd calendar: the "quarterly" dates aren't 90 days apart, and the January payment is the only one that lands in the following year. That's also why it's the most missed — it doesn't arrive with any of the year's other tax deadlines, it arrives in the dead of January when nobody is thinking about 2026.

The Safe Harbors: Your Escape Routes From the Penalty

You don't have to guess your income perfectly. Under the IRS estimated tax rules (Form 1040-ES; the penalty mechanics live in IRC §6654), you avoid the underpayment penalty entirely if your timely withholding plus estimated payments for 2026 meet any one of three thresholds:

  1. 90% of your 2026 tax (current-year liability, including self-employment tax), or
  2. 100% of your 2025 tax (prior-year safe harbor — payments can be made in four equal installments), or
  3. 110% of your 2025 tax if your 2025 adjusted gross income was over $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately).

Why would anyone use #2 or #3 when 90% is the smaller number? Because they're known. Your 2025 tax is a fixed number you can copy off last year's return; your 2026 tax is a forecast that can move. Freelancers with lumpy income often pay the prior-year safe harbor in four equal chunks and accept that a strong year means a bigger April bill — zero penalty, maximum certainty.

The catch that creates January 15 pressure: safe harbor payments must be timely. If you're using the prior-year (100%/110%) harbor and you skipped or shorted Q1–Q3, you cannot fix it with one giant January payment — the penalty is computed per quarter, on each quarter's shortfall, separately. The Q4 payment on January 15 protects you only for Q4.

One more trap: you had no tax liability last year (total tax zero, full 12-month year, US citizen/resident)? Then no estimated payments are required at all — no penalty regardless of what you owe in April. People who recently went from W-2 to self-employment often qualify for this in their first year and don't know it.

What the Penalty Costs

The federal underpayment penalty isn't a flat fine — it's interest, computed separately for each payment period (each "quarter"), on the shortfall for that period. The annualized rate floats with the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points and has been running at roughly 7% annualized in recent quarters (7% for the first three quarters of 2026). Daily accrual, no compounding grace period, no minimum like the late-filing penalty's $525 — but also no 25% cap, and it applies even if you're due a refund at filing.

On a $5,000 total shortfall spread across the year, that's on the order of $200–$350 in penalty depending on which quarters were short. Not catastrophic — but entirely avoidable, and it's pure interest to the IRS for being late on money you owed anyway.

Withholding enjoys a special superpower here: withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year regardless of when it was actually withheld. A December bonus with a big supplemental withholding rate, or a year-end W-4 bump, can retroactively cure earlier quarters. Estimated payments get no such treatment — each one only counts on its due date.

If You Owe New Jersey Too

NJ runs its own estimated tax system for residents with non-withheld income, and its bar is different: penalties apply when payments fall short of the lesser of 100% of your 2025 NJ tax or 80% of your 2026 NJ tax, with an expected-liability threshold of $400. NJ's underpayment rate for 2026 is a steeper 10% per year, charged monthly. The Q4 NJ payment for individuals is also due January 15, 2027 (NJ-1040-ES). If you file as a fiscal-year taxpayer or make payments through the year, verify your dates against the current NJ-1040-ES instructions — the state's calendar doesn't always mirror the federal one for every filing situation.

What To Do Before January 15

  1. Pull your 2025 total tax off your Form 1040 (line 22 area on the 2025 return). That number sets your cheapest certainty: 100% (or 110%) of it, in four equal installments.
  2. Add up what you've paid so far — withholding to date plus the April, June, and September estimates. Compare against 75% of your required annual payment (three of four installments, if you're on the equal-installment plan).
  3. Make the Q4 payment for the remaining quarter — IRS Direct Pay, EFTPS, or IRS Online Account, designated as a 2026 Form 1040-ES estimated payment (selecting the wrong year is the classic Direct Pay mistake).
  4. Never designate Q4 money as a "balance due" or 2027 payment — misapplied estimated payments generate penalty and interest notices that take months to unwind.
  5. If you're short on the prior-year harbor and your 2026 income is lower, the 90%-of-current-year harbor may be the smaller target — run both before sending money you may not need to.
