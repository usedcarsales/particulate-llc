The NJ Probate Checklist: 30 Steps to Settle a New Jersey Estate

October 11, 2026 · 9 min read · By Particulate LLC

When a parent dies in New Jersey, nobody hands the family a checklist. There are roughly thirty distinct steps between the death certificate and the final distribution — spread across the Surrogate's Court, the IRS, the DMV, banks, insurers, and sometimes a second state — and several of them have deadlines measured in days. The original Will has to reach the county Surrogate promptly. Social Security has to be notified so benefits stop. The estate needs its own EIN before a single dollar moves.

The order matters, because New Jersey executors are fiduciaries: distribute assets before creditors are paid and you can be held personally liable. So we built the checklist below. The First 10 Days: What Actually Has a Deadline

Most of the estate process is a marathon, but three things in the first week and a half are sprints:

  • The original Will. Whoever has custody of it is required to deliver it to the county Surrogate promptly after learning of the death — the 10-day mark is the commonly cited expectation. Photocopies don't count: probate requires the original signed document, with its witnesses.
  • Social Security. Benefits paid for the month of death and after must be returned. Notify SSA at 1-800-772-1213 immediately — an unnoticed continuing deposit becomes a clawback problem the executor inherits.
  • Certified death certificates. Every bank, insurer, the DMV, and the Surrogate want certified copies — never photocopies. Order more than you think you need: one per account, one per property, plus spares. Under-ordering is the most common early mistake because every later step queues behind it.

The Money That Never Enters Probate

Here's the part that surprises people: for many estates, the majority of money moves outside probate entirely. Jointly held property, payable-on-death bank accounts, life insurance with a named beneficiary, retirement accounts with a beneficiary on file, and anything titled to a living trust transfer directly — no Surrogate, no Letters, no waiting.

Two consequences follow:

  1. Beneficiary forms override the Will. An old form naming an ex-spouse still pays that ex-spouse, regardless of what the Will says. Annual designation reviews are the cheapest estate planning that exists.
  2. The probate estate can be small while the transfers are large. Don't equate "avoided probate" with "handled" — POD accounts still need a certified death certificate and a claim call, and they can still carry NJ inheritance tax consequences for non-spouse beneficiaries.

The Crypto Trap

Self-custodied cryptocurrency is the one asset class where "lost" is permanent. If the deceased held coins on a hardware wallet or an exchange and nobody can find the keys or the password manager, that value can be unrecoverable — no bank to call, no Surrogate certificate to present. Exchange-held crypto can follow normal estate processes, but you need the statements to know it exists. Check password managers, email recovery rules, and any seed-phrase documents early — it's a "Do first" item in the tool for a reason.

Why the Order Matters: Executor Liability

New Jersey executors and administrators are fiduciaries. The liability list is short and unforgiving: distributing assets before creditors are paid, missing tax filings (the final 1040, the NJ final return, and potentially the estate's own 1041 and NJ-1041), self-dealing, or ignoring the Will's terms. Each one can land on the executor personally. That's why the checklist puts creditor notice, the EIN, and the asset inventory ahead of distribution — every one of them is a shield, not just a formality.

Planning Ahead: The Other Tab

Switch the tool to "Planning ahead" and it flips to the five highest-leverage moves while everyone is still alive: a properly witnessed Will (NJ requires two witnesses; self-proving affidavits speed probate dramatically), financial and medical powers of attorney, an advance directive, a beneficiary-designation refresh, and a letter of instruction — the single most helpful document you can leave your family. The trust-funding check matters most of all: a trust only avoids probate for assets actually titled to it, and unfunded trusts are the most common estate-planning failure we see.
