NJ Executor Fee Calculator: What Does an Executor Get Paid in New Jersey?

October 13, 2026 · 8 min read · By Particulate LLC

Nobody becomes an executor for the money — but New Jersey does pay its executors, and the number surprises people in both directions. On a $500,000 estate, the statutory commission is $20,500. On a $1.5 million estate it's $48,000. And if you're co-executors with your sibling, the estate pays more than you'd expect — while each of you caps out below what a solo executor would earn.

It's all set by statute, which means it's all computable. How the NJ executor commission actually works

New Jersey executors earn two separate commissions. The corpus commission is the big one: a percentage of the probate estate value, on a statutory sliding scale that works exactly like tax brackets — each rate applies only to the portion inside that range.

So a $500,000 estate pays $10,000 on the first $200,000 plus $10,500 (3.5% of $300,000) — a $20,500 commission. A $1.5 million estate pays $10,000 + $28,000 + $10,000 = $48,000. The percentages fall as the estate grows, but the total keeps climbing: big estates still mean big commissions.

The income commission is separate: 6% of income the estate earns while you administer it — interest, dividends, rent from estate property. No court allowance needed. The classic mistake is applying it to a house sale: if an estate home worth $400,000 sells for $400,000, that money is corpus, not income — it was already counted. Income is only what the estate earns during administration.

The co-executor bump

When two or more executors serve, the estate pays an additional 1% of corpus for each executor beyond the first. On that $500,000 estate, one executor costs $20,500; two co-executors cost $20,500 + $5,000 = $25,500 total. Three cost $30,500.

The catch: no single co-executor may receive more than they'd have earned serving alone, and the statute adjusts each co-executor's share so adding names doesn't inflate anyone's take without limit. In practice co-executors split the total roughly evenly — the calculator shows the even split. Every extra executor costs the beneficiaries real money, which is worth remembering when a will names all three children "to keep the peace."

What counts as corpus (and what doesn't)

The commission runs only on probate assets — things that actually come into the executor's hands:

  • Counts: solely-owned bank and brokerage accounts, real estate titled in the decedent's name alone, solely-owned vehicles, personal property, business interests without a succession mechanism.
  • Doesn't count: life insurance paid to a named beneficiary, joint accounts with right of survivorship, retirement accounts with designated beneficiaries, payable-on-death accounts, assets already titled to a living trust.

One more wrinkle: if a prior executor resigned, died, or was removed after disposing of certain assets, the successor generally can't claim commission on what never came into their hands. An executor removed for cause loses commission rights entirely.

When the will names a different fee

If the will sets specific compensation — a flat amount, a different percentage, or "no commission" — that provision controls and is treated as full payment. The only way around it is a written renunciation of the will's compensation clause, filed with the Surrogate's Court or the clerk of the Superior Court before you qualify. This matters most for wills drafted decades ago with fees that have since gone stale: read the compensation clause before accepting the appointment.

The tax trap: why beneficiary-executors often waive

Here's the counterintuitive part: many executors should turn the fee down. Inheritance is generally not subject to income tax — but a commission is fully taxable income to the executor. If you're inheriting 100% of the estate, taking a $20,500 commission converts $20,500 of tax-free inheritance into taxable income, and the estate's deduction only partially offsets it depending on size and brackets.

The waiver math flips when your beneficial share is small or zero: then the commission is genuinely earned pay for genuinely performed work, and waiving it is giving money away. Professional executors always take the fee. Run both scenarios — or ask us to run them for you — before filing the estate accounting.

Extraordinary pay, and what happens if you overcharge

The statutory scale covers ordinary administration: collecting assets, paying debts, filing returns, distributing. Litigation on behalf of the estate, running a decedent's business, or complex tax disputes can justify additional compensation under N.J.S.A. 3B:18-16 — but you must petition the court, and "time-consuming" alone doesn't qualify; the work must be genuinely unusual for an estate of that size.

Courts review fees when beneficiaries object (Surrogate's Court or the Chancery Division, Probate Part), and they examine records. Take more than authorized and you can be ordered to repay with interest; serious misuse invites surcharge or worse. Executors who also serve as the estate's attorney may collect both the commission and attorney's fees under N.J.S.A. 3B:18-6 — but that dual billing draws exactly the scrutiny you'd expect, so document which hat each hour was worn under.

Payment order protects you: commissions are administration expenses with high priority under N.J.S.A. 3B:22-2 — funeral costs first, administration (including your commission) next, then creditors, beneficiaries last.
