NJ Paycheck Calculator: What You Actually Take Home in 2026

October 8, 2026 · 8 min read · By Particulate LLC

You negotiated $75,000. The offer letter looks great. Then the first direct deposit lands and it's about $2,269 — not the $2,885 gross you were mentally spending. That gap is six different taxes and deductions, and in New Jersey one of them works differently than almost anywhere else in the country.

Most online paycheck calculators get New Jersey wrong. They apply a generic "state income tax" model and miss the part that actually costs NJ residents money: New Jersey taxes your 401(k) contributions. The calculator below runs the real 2026 rules — federal brackets, NJ's own rate tables, FICA, and that 401(k) quirk — so the number it shows is the number your payroll department is aiming for.

The NJ Quirk Most Calculators Miss

New Jersey's income tax on wages looks like a state version of the federal system, but four differences change your paycheck:

  • No standard deduction. NJ taxes your gross wages directly. You get a $1,000 personal exemption ($2,000 married filing jointly), plus $1,500 if you or your spouse is 65+ or disabled — that's it. Compare that to the federal $16,100 standard deduction and you see why NJ bites harder than its rate table suggests.
  • Your 401(k) does NOT reduce NJ tax. Federal law exempts 401(k) deferrals from federal income tax. New Jersey taxes them now anyway. A $4,500 contribution saves you $0 in NJ state tax this year.
  • Section 125 medical premiums ARE exempt. Pre-tax health premiums come out before both federal and NJ tax — one of the few deductions NJ respects.
  • Dependent credit: $500 per dependent under 18, credited directly against NJ tax — not a deduction. For a family with two young kids, that's up to $1,000 off the NJ bill.

FICA: The Lines You Can't Change

Social Security takes 6.2% of wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500 — above that, the employee side drops to zero for the rest of the year. Medicare has no cap: 1.45% on everything, plus an extra 0.9% above $200,000 single / $250,000 joint. Unlike income tax, FICA ignores your deductions entirely — your 401(k) deferral is still fully taxed for Social Security and Medicare purposes.

Self-employed? The math doubles.

If you're freelancing or running a side business, you pay both halves: 15.3% SE tax on 92.35% of net earnings (the same $184,500 Social Security cap applies). The good news: half of your SE tax is deductible against federal income tax. Switch the calculator to "Self-Employed" and the SE tax line replaces the Social Security/Medicare lines so you can see the real damage on contract income.
