When Should You Claim Social Security? The $75,000 Difference Hiding in Your Claiming Age

October 10, 2026 · 10 min read · By Particulate LLC

62 is the single most common claiming age for both men and women (SSA's own statistics say so) — and it is also the most expensive decision many retirees ever make without doing the math. For a worker with a $2,500 full benefit (PIA), claiming at 62 instead of 70 means a check that is 43.5% smaller, every month, for life: $1,750 instead of $3,100.

The other side of that trade is real too: the early claimer collects eight years of checks the delayer never sees — $168,000 in the example above. Delaying is only "free money" if you live long enough. The break-even lands at age 80.3 — and that number moves with your investment returns, your taxes, and your longevity.

Most claiming-age articles hand you a rule ("wait if you're healthy!") and stop. The calculator below runs the actual math: your birth year's full retirement age, the 5/9-of-1% monthly reduction, the 8%-per-year delayed credits, the 2026 earnings test ($24,480 / $65,160), and the federal "tax torpedo" bands — all live, all recomputed as you type. Pull your PIA from ssa.gov → my Social Security first; guessing it produces confident-looking garbage.

What the Calculator Is Actually Telling You

The engine behind the widget is straightforward Social Security arithmetic, and it's worth understanding rather than trusting:

  • The early-claiming haircut is 5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months before your full retirement age, then 5/12 of 1% for months beyond that. For a worker with FRA 67 (born 1960+), claiming at 62 means 60 months early: 36 × 5/9% + 24 × 5/12% = a 30% reduction. Claim at 62 with a $2,500 PIA and you lock in $1,750/month for life.
  • Delayed credits are 2/3 of 1% per month (8% per year) from FRA to 70. Same worker waiting to 70: 36 × 2/3% = +24%, so $3,100/month.
  • Claiming at 62 is a 43.5% smaller check than claiming at 70 — a gap of $1,350/month in this example. That is the "cost" side of delaying that the head-start $168,000 has to justify.

The assumption that flips the answer: your real return

Nominal break-evens flatter delaying. The last table column tells the real story: at a 4% real return, the early claimer who invests the checks is actually ahead by about $27,575 at 85 ($792,998 vs $765,423) — because money collected at 62 compounds for 23 years. Drop the return assumption to 0% real and the ranking flips: the 70-claim wins by the full $75,000.

The honest summary: delaying is longevity insurance, not a return optimization. If you expect to live past ~80 — and a healthy 62-year-old usually should bet that way — the bigger check is the safer bet. If your family history says otherwise, or your portfolio reliably beats inflation after tax, claiming earlier is not a mistake.

Three Traps the Calculator Surfaces

1. The earnings test punishes early claiming while working

Claim before FRA while still earning and SSA withholds $1 of benefits per $2 over the 2026 limit of $24,480 ($65,160 in the year you reach FRA, at $1 per $3, for months before FRA only). Example: claim at 63 while making $40,000 — SSA withholds about $7,760, roughly four months of checks. The money isn't lost (it's recalculated back into your benefit at FRA), but early claiming while working is often the worst of both worlds: a permanently reduced benefit and withholding.

2. The tax torpedo: claiming late can newly expose benefits to tax

Up to 85% of Social Security becomes taxable as "provisional income" (other income + half your benefit) crosses $25,000 single / $32,000 joint — 50% taxable above the base, up to 85% above $34,000 / $44,000. The interaction is nonlinear and can run against delay: a single retiree with only $10,000 of other income pays 0% tax on benefits if claiming at 62 (provisional $20,500), but lands in the 50% band if claiming at 70 (provisional $28,600) — because the bigger benefit itself pushes them over. Run both ages in the calculator with your real income before assuming "delayed = always better after tax."

3. Couples: the survivor check is the number that matters

When one spouse dies, the survivor receives the larger of the two benefits — not both. That means the higher earner delaying to 70 permanently raises what the survivor will collect, often for a decade or more. The 62/70 split (lower earner early, higher earner to 70) frequently beats both-spouses-at-FRA over two lifetimes. This calculator models one worker only — couples should model the survivor scenario explicitly before filing.

Before You File: the 60-Second Checklist

  1. Pull your official statement at ssa.gov → my Social Security and confirm your real PIA and FRA — every number above is anchored to them.
  2. If claiming before FRA while working: apply the 2026 earnings-test limits ($24,480 / $65,160) to your expected income.
  3. Map your provisional income (other income + 50% of SS) against the $25k/$32k and $34k/$44k taxability thresholds at each candidate age.
  4. Couples: compare survivor benefits at 62 vs 70 for the higher earner before either of you files.
  5. Set the return slider to what you actually expect after inflation — not the bull-market number — and read the break-even against your own longevity read.

Claiming is effectively irreversible (a narrow 12-month do-over window aside), and these heuristics don't cover WEP/GPO, spousal coordination, or state tax interactions. A professional review of your actual record is cheap insurance on a six-figure decision.
