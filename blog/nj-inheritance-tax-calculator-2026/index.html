NJ Inheritance Tax Calculator 2026: Who Actually Owes (and Who Doesn't)

October 20, 2026 · Particulate LLC · 9-minute read

Ask most people about death taxes in New Jersey and you'll hear that NJ is "the worst state to die in." That nickname was earned decades ago — and half of it is now obsolete. Here's the 2026 reality, straight from the New Jersey Division of Taxation:

  • Estate tax: $0. No New Jersey estate tax is imposed on the estates of residents dying on or after January 1, 2018. If your aunt's neighbor told you the state takes a cut of the whole estate, that neighbor's information is at least eight years old.
  • Inheritance tax: alive and well. What NJ kept is a tax on the beneficiary, not the estate. Whether you owe depends entirely on your relationship to the person who died. Spouse, kids, grandkids, parents, grandparents: $0. Sibling: 11–16% on amounts above $25,000. Niece, nephew, friend, partner: 15–16% from the first $700,000 and 16% above.
The confusion is expensive. Families routinely assume one of two wrong things: "NJ taxes everything" (false — most beneficiaries pay nothing) or "NJ abolished its death tax" (false — it only abolished the estate half). Estate tax vs inheritance tax: the two-minute primer

The two taxes sound interchangeable and are almost opposites:

FederalApplies only above the $15M per-person exemption (2026, set by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 — no sunset)None — the federal government has no inheritance tax

So when a website or assistant tells you "New Jersey's estate tax exemption is $675,000" or warns about a sunset cutting the federal exemption back to ~$5 million at the end of 2025 — that content predates the two biggest changes in this area: NJ's 2018 estate tax repeal, and the 2025 federal law that permanently set the exemption at $15 million per person ($30M per couple). The sunset everyone feared for eight years did not happen.

For almost every New Jersey family, the federal estate tax is now irrelevant — $15M is far above the typical estate. The tax that actually bites is the state inheritance tax, and it can bite on small estates, because it has no minimum estate size. A $150,000 bank account left entirely to a nephew owes roughly $22,500.

The four beneficiary classes (official rates)

New Jersey sorts every beneficiary into a class, and the class — not the estate — sets the bill. From the Division of Taxation's rate schedule:

Two traps hide in the class definitions:

  • The "sibling vs everyone else" cliff: a brother pays 11% above $25K; a nephew pays 15% from dollar one of the first $700K. Same family, same asset, materially different tax — pure relationship classification.
  • Marriage changes the class, not just the exemption: a long-term unmarried partner is Class D (15–16%) while a spouse is Class A ($0). A daughter-in-law is Class C; if the marriage ends, she drops to Class D.

Why the tax bill arrives with an 8-month clock

The inheritance tax return (Form IT-R for New Jersey residents) and payment are due within 8 months of the date of death. But the deadline families actually feel is enforced by the banks: financial institutions and county recording offices won't release or transfer most assets without a waiver from the Division of Taxation (or Form L-8, the self-executing waiver available when all beneficiaries are Class A). In practice:

  • All Class A + spouse: Form L-8 can release bank accounts quickly — no tax due, minimal wait.
  • Any Class C or D beneficiary: the estate must file the return, pay the tax, and wait for the Division to issue waivers before frozen accounts and real estate transfers clear. Real money, sitting behind paperwork, for months.
  • Real estate: Form L-9 handles the waiver for the primary residence when it passes to a Class A beneficiary (typically the surviving spouse).

What actually counts as "received"

The tax base isn't just the bank account. NJ looks broadly at transfers at death, including:

  • Jointly held assets: the decedent's share of joint accounts with a Class C/D co-owner is generally taxable (joint accounts with a spouse are Class A).
  • Gifts made within 3 years of death: NJ presumes certain transfers "in contemplation of death" are taxable — one of the few places gifting doesn't cleanly escape.
  • TOD/POD designations and trusts: a payable-on-death account naming a nephew is a Class D transfer even though it never passes through probate. The inheritance tax follows the beneficiary, not the probate process.

That last point is why this tax sneaks up on families: avoiding probate (with TOD designations, trusts, or joint titling) avoids the probate process, not the inheritance tax. A fully "probate-free" estate can still owe in full.

Five moves that change the number

  1. Check the class before you plan, not after. The difference between "my sibling" and "my sibling's kid" as beneficiary on the same asset is 11–16% vs 15–16% with a smaller exemption — or vs 0% if a Class A child stands between them.
  2. Pay the tax early if you can. Interest runs on unpaid inheritance tax, and waivers release assets once the Division is satisfied — paying sooner unfreezes the estate sooner.
  3. File on time even if unsure. An accurate return with payment within 8 months avoids penalties and interest stacking on top of the tax itself.
  4. Charitable residuary: bequests to Class E entities are exempt — a charitable remainder gift reduces the taxable shares of Class C/D beneficiaries dollar for dollar.
  5. Get the marital steps right: assets passing outright to a surviving spouse are Class A ($0). Complex second-marriage plans that route money through trusts should be reviewed with a professional, because the class of the ultimate recipient is what the Division cares about.

The federal side in 2026 (the part that changed)

For completeness: the federal estate tax exemption for deaths in 2026 is $15 million per person (indexed for inflation starting in 2027), set permanently by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed July 4, 2025. The annual gift exclusion for 2026 is $19,000 per recipient. The widely-feared "TCJA sunset" — which would have dropped the exemption to roughly $5–7 million in 2026 — did not occur, which is why pre-2025 estate-planning content (and plenty of AI summaries trained on it) is now wrong on the numbers.

For estates under $15M per person, the practical planning question in New Jersey is no longer "how do I dodge the federal estate tax" — it's "how do my beneficiaries' classes come out, and does the 8-month clock get handled properly." That's a much smaller, much more fixable problem.
