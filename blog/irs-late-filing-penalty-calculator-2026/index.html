IRS Late Filing & Late Payment Penalty Calculator: What Missing October 15 Costs

October 14, 2026 · 7 min read · By Particulate LLC

The October 15 extension deadline is tomorrow, and the IRS punishes missing it faster than almost any other mistake in the tax system. File late and the failure-to-file penalty runs at 5% of your unpaid tax per month, up to 25%. Can't pay on top of that? The failure-to-pay penalty adds 0.5% per month on the same money — combined, a net 5% per month while both clocks run, because the IRS credits the pay penalty against the file penalty. And interest at 7% compounded daily sits on top of all of it.

On a $10,000 unpaid tax bill, every month of not filing costs about $500. Miss the deadline by 60 days and a $525 minimum penalty locks in even if you owe almost nothing. How the two penalty clocks actually work

The IRS charges two separate penalties for two separate failures, and they interact in a way most people get wrong:

PenaltyRateRuns onCapStops when
Failure to file5% / month (or partial month)Unpaid tax after timely payments & credits25% (5 months)You file the return
Failure to pay0.5% / month (or partial month)Same unpaid tax25% (~50 months)You pay in full

Because both penalties run on the same dollars in the same months, IRC §6651(c) subtracts the failure-to-pay amount from the failure-to-file charge — that's why the combined rate is a net 5% per month, not the 5.5% you'd get by adding the gross rates. After five months the failure-to-file penalty maxes out (22.5% net of offset, plus the 2.5% of pay penalty that ran alongside it — 25% of the unpaid tax in those months), but the failure-to-pay penalty keeps running at 0.5% per month until it hits its own 25% cap years later. The clocks are ruthless about partial months: file one day into a new month and that month counts in full.

The $525 trap at 60 days

Here's the rule that catches people with small balances: for returns due after December 31, 2025, once your return is more than 60 days late, the failure-to-file penalty becomes the greater of the calculated amount or $525 (or 100% of the unpaid tax, if less). Owe $300 in tax? The minimum doesn't apply — but owe $2,000 and go 60+ days past the deadline, and you're paying $525 for the privilege even though the calculated 5%-per-month figure might be lower.

This is why the universal advice is: always file something. An extension you can't fully pay, a return with a balance due you'll set up payments on, even a return you know has errors — filing stops the 5%-per-month clock entirely and caps your exposure at the far cheaper 0.5%-per-month pay penalty plus interest.

Interest: the part nobody budgets for

Penalties aren't the end. Underpayment interest runs from the original April due date until the balance is gone — including through any payment plan — at the federal rate: short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, adjusted quarterly. For the quarter beginning October 1, 2026, that's 7% per year, compounded daily for individuals. Compounded daily adds a few tenths of a percent over the year versus simple interest, and interest runs on penalties once they're assessed too.

The compounding is why tax debt feels like it grows faster than the stated rates. A balance left alone for a year at 7% compounded daily grows about 7.25% — on top of penalties that may have added 25% or more.

If you can't pay: the half-price escape hatch

The penalty system has a built-in reward for staying in compliance:

  • File on time, pay later: zero failure-to-file penalty. You carry only the 0.5%/month pay penalty plus interest while you work it off.
  • Approved installment agreement (for individuals who filed on time): the failure-to-pay penalty drops from 0.5% to 0.25% per month — half price — for as long as the plan is active and you stay current.
  • Intent-to-levy notice: ignore the IRS long enough and the pay penalty jumps to 1% per month — the escalation the 0.25% relief exists to prevent.
  • Reasonable cause / first-time relief: penalties can be abated for documented reasonable cause, and the IRS has long offered administrative first-time relief for taxpayers with a clean three-year compliance history — ask before assuming you're stuck.

The sequencing matters: file first, then arrange payment. The reverse order — paying without filing — leaves the 5%-per-month clock running on whatever's still due.
